# 01 — Vision and experience

[Documentation index](README.md) · [Next: world, time, and control](02-world-time-and-control.md)

## High concept

Kestrum is a node-based war simulation about emergent campaigns and characters across generations. The player guides a new kingdom from a headquarters into a world of other growing powers. Armies travel through connected cities, forts, settlements, bridges, passes, ports, shrines, and wilderness. Important regions contain their own connected locations, so taking a province can require several engagements and a sustained supply route.

Most people begin as ordinary troops, squires, recruits, neophytes, or apprentices. Service, opportunity, mentors, resources, equipment, survival, success, and failure shape their careers. Enemy factions develop people through the same broad rules. Someone who escapes an early defeat may return decades later as a renowned commander.

Over those decades, an outpost may become a capital; a fortress may become a ruin; a founder may retire while a student takes command. The player should recognize the causes of these changes in their own decisions.

## Player role and priorities

**Working interpretation:** the player controls the continuing kingdom, rather than embodying one mortal ruler. Characters can age or die while the campaign continues. The exact fiction of sovereign succession remains open; a dynasty is one possible source of continuity.

The guiding hierarchy is:

1. Watching history emerge.
2. War and territorial strategy.
3. Developing characters through opportunities.
4. Growing a kingdom.
5. Families and succession supporting that longer history.

The player chooses where to commit forces, how to compose armies, what conditions to create for growth, and when to accept risk. Outcomes should remain attributable to geography, preparation, people, and events. A stronger roster alone should not solve a war fought across too many fronts.

## Design pillars

### Connected space creates strategy

Routes, chokepoints, ownership, reinforcement paths, and supply determine where armies can act. Armies occupy actual nodes. Committing all experienced people to an eastern offensive leaves the western border exposed.

### Important places become campaigns

A province, city, or fortress complex can contain an internal node network. Several friendly and hostile armies can operate there at once. Partial control, isolated resistance, and regional entry points keep the region meaningful throughout an invasion.

### Experience creates identity

Disposition influences behavior, but experience, mentorship, opportunity, and consequences shape the person. The player sees evidence of courage or leadership before a trait formally recognizes it. Recruitment does not disclose a complete personality as a table of hidden numerical attributes.

### Ordinary people become significant

A notable person emerges from meaningful service. The confirmed army model uses six troop-formation slots with abstract members who can become individually tracked after distinguishing themselves. A plausible recent history grounds the emerging hero without simulating every soldier from birth.

### Rivals belong to the same world

Enemy factions recruit, develop infrastructure, gain experience, recover, retreat, and nurture specialists under broadly equivalent constraints. Their recurring commanders have histories rather than appearing as unexplained difficulty upgrades.

### Places and people keep changing

Settlements develop unevenly and can decline. Age changes a character's useful role. Destruction, retirement, and death have consequences that survive the immediate event.

### Continuity outlives individuals

Children, siblings, students, squires, trusted officers, adopted wards, political heirs, and religious disciples can carry a legacy. Having children is optional. Mentorship must be equally capable of making a life matter.

### Long wars have rhythm

Offensives, stalemates, winter pauses, rebuilding, border raids, political interruptions, truces, and renewed invasions make a decades-long conflict believable. A long war is not one uninterrupted assault on the same castle.

## Inspirations and boundaries

| Inspiration | Relevant influence |
| --- | --- |
| Stellaris | A broad world of competing powers growing from small starts; major regions as strategic hubs |
| Total War: Warhammer | Sequential faction turns, distinct armies, and a light persistent siege model |
| Suikoden | Important characters as figureheads within larger military forces |
| Unicorn Overlord | Composition and preparation followed by automatic combat resolution |

These are design references from the sources, not specifications to reproduce those games. Kestrum keeps settlement management light, diplomacy initially simple, and combat automatic. It should avoid a heavy city builder, detailed political simulation, mandatory dynasty optimization, and manual control of individual soldiers.

## Core loops

### A turn

Inspect fronts and known threats → select an army or place → assess routes, costs, and supply → move, recruit, reorganize, develop, or fight → review consequences → finish the faction turn.

### A campaign

Recruit troops → compose armies → move through the node map → fight and preserve survivors → gain formation experience → recognize emerging characters → improve or divide armies → expand and establish outposts → change settlements and borders → face larger wars.

### Across decades

Explore → establish → defend → develop → expand → fight → rebuild → age → succeed → repeat. Each cycle leaves people, institutions, ruins, relationships, equipment, names, and memories behind.

```text
Map and routes -> Campaign choices -> Experiences -> Characters
      ^                                      |          |
      |                                      v          v
Changing places <------ Consequences <----- Armies and history
```

## Strategic tensions

| Decision | Benefit and cost to weigh |
| --- | --- |
| Concentrate or spread | Several heroes strengthen one army; distributing them covers more fronts |
| Build roads or protect the frontier | Roads speed reinforcement and can also speed an invader |
| Preserve veterans or risk them | Veterans are effective and can produce heroes; destruction ends that formation |
| Expand or consolidate | Outposts create opportunities and additional territory to defend |
| Assault or maintain a siege | Early assault accepts stronger defenses; delay lets relief forces gather |
| Promote or retain | An emerging commander can lead another army at the cost of weakening their old host |
| Continue service or change role | A veteran can still matter through command, governance, training, or mentorship |

## Setting and botanical identity

The setting can use flowers and plants in heraldry, noble houses, orders, regions, fortresses, classes, titles, and historical conflicts. This is cultural symbolism; plants are not the combatants.

Source examples include Roseguard, Blackthorn Company, Laurel Captain, Hawthorn Keep, Ashmere, White Rose Gate, the War of Three Blooms, and the Red Laurel Campaign. Distinct botanical emblems can help identify factions and armies on the map. Dragonkin, drakes, dragons, wyverns, clerics, mages, and religious institutions establish room for fantasy without fixing a full bestiary or cosmology.

**Proposal:** botanical symbols should identify cultures and institutions consistently. They should not require every common noun or message to become a floral metaphor. Species, cultures, visual art direction, audio, and naming vocabularies remain content decisions.

## Intended player experience

The prototype should produce observations such as:

- “I'm keeping this squire with Serai because he's turning into a good cavalry officer.”
- “Don't send Mira there. I need her dealing with the outbreak in Rosemarch.”
- “That enemy knight again? He survived the last campaign.”
- “I can't abandon Hawthorn because Tomas has been holding it for three battles.”

Later, the player should recognize that a capital was once empty plains, a ruin guarded the frontier for twenty years, a commander learned from a founder, and a region's current importance follows decisions made decades earlier.

**Proposal — evaluation:** ask players to explain one remembered person, one place, and one strategic reversal using events that actually occurred. If they can only describe numerical upgrades, the history and presentation systems need work.
